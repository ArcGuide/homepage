How do I cold email a professor at another university for remote research?

A template and the common mistakes for cold emailing a professor at another university about remote research, from five faculty and campus guides.

STARC Institute · 2026-11-12 · Follow @Starc_Institute on X

Short answer

To cold email a professor at another university for remote research, write to one person about one specific piece of their work, say up front which school you attend and what year you are, ask for something small (a short call, or whether remote undergraduates can join), and attach a one-page resume. Make the remote part concrete: hours per week, time zone, the skills you can use without being in the lab. Follow up once in the same thread, then move on.

How do you cold email a professor at another university for remote research?

Most cold-email advice for undergraduates assumes you're writing to someone down the hall. Your school's research office can help, the professor may feel some duty to its own students, and if the email works you'll walk into the lab next week.

Writing to a professor at a different university, for work you'd do remotely, removes all three. You're a stranger, the professor has no obligation to you, and you're asking them to supervise someone they may never meet. That doesn't make it a bad idea. It means the email has to do more work.

We read five guides written by faculty and campus offices: Philip Resnik's "How to email a professor", Michael Guerzhoy's page on cold emails, the University of Illinois Grainger College's undergraduate research guide, a Lawrence University career center post and a Teachers College, Columbia IRB blog post. They agree on most of the basics. Almost none of them say anything specific about remote work at another school, so that part below is our own advice, labeled as such.

What do faculty and campus guides agree on?

Write to one person, about their work. Resnik's first rule is that the professor should know the email was written to them, not to a list; he notes that a bulk-style greeting is what mass emails look like. The Illinois guide says to spell the professor's name correctly and address them as Professor plus last name. Guerzhoy goes further: the easiest way to show you understood a paper, he writes, is to suggest how it might be extended.

Show you can do something. Guerzhoy frames the whole email as answering one question: do you understand the work, and can you get things done? He suggests describing, briefly, something difficult you actually did, ideally with a link. The Illinois template has a paragraph for one or two experiences from your resume.

Ask for something small. Resnik suggests asking for a conversation, or, failing that, a pointer to colleagues who might take a student. The Lawrence post suggests asking whether the professor has time for a 20 to 30 minute meeting.

Keep it short and attach a resume. The Teachers College post says to keep the email short and attach a resume or CV. The Illinois guide says "A 1-page resume will do just fine," and treats a transcript and project links as optional.

Where do they disagree, or stay silent?

Follow-up timing. Illinois says three to five business days, in the same thread, and warns against sending several separate emails. Teachers College says one to two weeks. Both are reasonable; a professor at another school owes you less, so we'd wait toward a week.

Where the resume goes. Resnik doesn't say skip it. He says don't lead with it: mention it at the end as extra information. The Illinois template, by contrast, puts your experience in the body. The two fit together: one sentence about what you did, the resume for the rest.

AI-written emails. Only Guerzhoy addresses this, and he's direct about it: "I strongly suggest not generating the whole thing with an LLM." He lists the signs he notices, such as unusually smooth prose with no rough edges and praise for a minor result. The other four don't mention it.

Other universities and remote work. This is the gap. The Illinois guide is explicitly about labs on its own campus. Resnik mentions "some other" institution once, without separate advice. Only the Lawrence post addresses it directly, telling students emailing off campus to name their school. A separate Lawrence post on finding experience during the pandemic describes remote projects as a way to build computational skills when in-person research isn't possible. Nobody we read says how often professors accept remote undergraduates from other schools. We don't know either.

A template you can adapt

This is our own template, built from the points above. Rewrite every line for the person you're writing to; a template sent unchanged to ten professors is the mistake Resnik and others warn about.

Subject: Undergraduate question about your [specific paper or project] Dear Professor [Last name], I'm a [year] studying [major] at [your university]. I read your [year] paper on [topic], and I wondered whether [one specific extension or question, in a sentence]. I'd like to ask whether your group ever works with undergraduates remotely. I can offer [N] hours a week this [term], I'm in [time zone], and I've [one concrete thing you did, with a link: a repo, an analysis, a write-up]. Would you have 20 minutes for a call in the next few weeks? If remote work isn't possible for your group, I'd be grateful for the name of anyone you think might be a fit. My one-page resume is attached in case it's useful. Thank you for reading this; I know you get many of these. [Name] [University email, link]

Why these choices: the subject line names their work, not "Research opportunity," so it doesn't look like every other inquiry in the inbox. Your school and year come in the first line, as the Lawrence post advises. The remote part is concrete because "remote" with no hours or skills attached is easy to decline.

The mistakes that sink these emails

A checklist before you hit send. The first five come from the guides above; the last four are mostly our own reasoning about remote, cross-school requests.

  • Generic greeting, or the same email to many professors.
  • Praise without specifics ("your fascinating research").
  • Opening with "please find my resume attached."
  • Several separate follow-ups instead of one reply in the same thread.
  • An email that reads as fully AI-written.
  • Not saying which university you attend.
  • Asking for "any research opportunity" instead of naming what you could do from a distance: code, data analysis, literature review, reproducing a figure.
  • No hours, term or time zone, so the professor has to ask.
  • Requesting a letter, a paper or a title before any work exists.

Also consider that some research can't be done remotely: wet labs, human-subjects work with restricted data, or projects that need lab computers. If a professor says no for those reasons, that's not about you.

How STARC handles this

Our research track skips the cold email. You leave a research profile; we contact you only when there's a real match with a researcher who has room, which usually isn't the case. Then comes a short trial task, and a milestone agreed up front. Publication is never promised. The short version

Name your school, name their work, name what you can do from where you are, and ask for twenty minutes. Follow up once. Then write to someone else.

Related questions

Is it okay to email a professor at a different university?

Yes. A faculty guide by Philip Resnik writes for students emailing a professor at their own institution "or some other one", and a Lawrence University career center post tells students emailing off campus to name their school. A stranger at another school owes you nothing, so plan for some silence.

How long should I wait before following up?

The guides we read disagree. A University of Illinois guide says three to five business days; a Teachers College, Columbia post says one to two weeks. For a professor you have no tie to, the longer end is the safer choice. Follow up once, in the same thread.

Can I use ChatGPT to write the email?

Michael Guerzhoy, who writes about cold emails on his faculty site, says trying a language model is a reasonable start but strongly advises against generating the whole email. He lists very smooth, generic prose as a sign of an AI-written message.

Should I attach my resume?

Most guides say yes, one page. Resnik's point is placement: mention it at the end, not in your opening line.
