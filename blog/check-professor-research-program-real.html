How do I check if a professor in a paid research program is real?

Four checks for the professor named in a paid research program: identity, title, recent work, and whether they will actually work with you.

STARC Institute · 2026-11-05 · Follow @Starc_Institute on X

Short answer

To check if a professor in a paid research program is real, find them yourself on the university's own site, read their exact title, look for recent work in your topic, then contact them through a university address you found on your own. A real faculty page is only the first step. The questions that matter more are whether they agreed to be in this program and how many hours they'll actually spend with you.

How do I check if a professor in a paid research program is real?

A program's page shows a headshot, a famous university and the word "Professor." You're being asked to commit months of your time and maybe a lot of money on the strength of that name. So how do you check?

We looked for an official guide to this exact question and didn't find one. We found plenty from colleges on research opportunities, and almost nothing on vetting the person named in a paid program. What does exist comes from nearby places: university rules on using their names, a career center's guide to faculty titles, ORCID's own notes on what its records prove, and federal and campus warnings about scammers posing as professors.

Put together, they point to one conclusion. "Is this professor real?" is really four questions, and the first is the easiest to check.

The four checks, in order

CheckWhat to doWhat a pass tells you
IdentityFind them from the university's home page, not a link the program gave youThis person exists and works there
TitleRead the exact title, department and datesWhat the role really is
ActivityFind work from the last two or three years, and read one abstractThey're doing research, and it's near your topic
InvolvementContact them yourself, and ask the program for hours in writingThey agreed to this, and they'll work with you

A real faculty page only clears the first row. Keep going.

What does the title actually mean?

"Professor at a top university" can cover very different jobs. A University of Connecticut career guide warns that every institution classifies ranks in its own way, so a title at one school can't be assumed to mean the same at another. It lays out a typical ladder from instructor up to professor, and notes that "visiting" signals a temporary role and that adjunct faculty are usually part-time, often paid per course.

None of those is a bad job, and an adjunct can be a wonderful teacher. The check is narrower: does the program's page match what the university says? Watch for these gaps:

  • "Professor" on the ad, but "adjunct," "lecturer" or "visiting" on the university's page.
  • A university named in the present tense, for someone who left years ago.
  • A department that has nothing to do with your project.
  • A common name. Confirm the department and research area, since the person on the faculty page may not be the person in the ad.

Does the university stand behind the program?

You can't assume so. This is easy to miss, because a logo-heavy page suggests the school is involved.

University rules draw this line themselves. Penn's policy lets faculty mention their affiliation in personal activities such as consulting, if the title is accurate and nothing implies that Penn endorses the activity. It also bars university insignia in personal activities. Harvard Medical School is tighter: faculty should stick to their formal titles, use them only in outside work tied to their expertise, and keep them in biographical text rather than visually linking the school to the outside activity.

The two schools differ on the details and agree on the principle. A professor can be entirely real and the university can have nothing to do with the program. Rules also vary by school, and we can't tell you how any one program follows them. But a university's seal on a private program's page is worth one email to that department.

Two reasonable questions for the program: "Is this run by the university, or by a private company with a professor attached?" and "Which legal entity am I signing with?"

How do you know they're active, and that it's them?

Recent work is the simplest signal. Search the name with the university on Google Scholar and in journals, look for papers from the last two or three years, and read one abstract. If the project you've been offered has no link to anything they've published, ask why.

Profiles prove less than they appear to. ORCID explains that people can add affiliations to their own records by hand, and that an entry carries more weight when a member organization adds it, because that organization is vouching for the link. So check who added the entry. ORCID also says that a record without those markers isn't necessarily a problem, and that a valid record can be entirely self-reported. A strong entry is useful evidence. A weak or missing one proves nothing.

Then there's impersonation, a different risk. A Detroit Free Press report describes scammers using real professors' names in fake job and research-assistant offers to students, and cites a University of Michigan warning that they spoof email addresses. The report is about job scams, not paid programs, but its top tip from an FTC official carries over: reach the person through contact details you found on your own.

A short note to the address on the faculty page can settle a lot:

Hello Professor Lee, I'm considering [program name], which lists you as a mentor for [project]. Could you confirm that you're involved, and whether you'd meet with students directly? Thank you.

A busy professor may not reply, so silence isn't proof of anything. A reply saying "I've never heard of them" is.

What to get in writing before you commit

Sources go quiet here, so this list is our own advice. A real professor can still be a name on a brochure. Ask the program:

  1. Who runs each meeting, and who reviews my work?
  2. How many hours will I have directly with the named professor, in total?
  3. If that person becomes unavailable, what happens?
  4. Can I speak with the professor before I commit?
  5. Can you connect me with a past participant I choose myself?

Clear answers count for a lot. If the program is vague, or says the mentor is assigned only after you've signed, weigh that heavily. See also our checklist on whether paid research internships are worth it.

How does this apply to STARC?

Apply the same test to us. STARC doesn't list team names yet, as our How it works page says, and by the logic of this post that's a real limit on what you can verify. We'd rather say so than dress it up. So judge us by the rules we've written down: we won't claim mentors, partners or results we don't have, and we don't sell authorship or letters.

Our research track opens only when an active researcher genuinely has room. It starts with a short trial task, so both sides can see whether it works, and publication is never promised. Our main program, the one-person company cohort, doesn't rest on any big name: you're judged by a live product and honest build logs. The first cohort hasn't run yet, so there are no results to show. The FAQ covers the rest.

The short version

Find the professor yourself, read the exact title, look for recent work near your topic, and contact them through an address you found on your own. Then ask how many hours you'll really get. A real name on a program page answers only the first of those.

Related questions

Does a .edu email address prove it's really the professor?

No. A University of Michigan warning described scammers spoofing real professors' email addresses. Find the address or phone number yourself on the university's site, then start a new message rather than replying.

Does a university's name in the ad mean the university runs the program?

No. Penn lets faculty mention their affiliation in personal activities like consulting, as long as it doesn't imply university endorsement. Check the university's own site for the program, or ask the department.

What if the professor has no Google Scholar or ORCID profile?

That alone means little. Profiles are optional, and ORCID says a record missing its trust markers isn't necessarily a problem. Look for papers on the university page or in journals instead.

Is it a bad sign if a graduate student runs the weekly meetings?

Not by itself. A good graduate student can be an excellent mentor. It's only a problem if you were sold a professor and got someone else, so ask who runs each meeting before you commit.
