What should parents check before paying for a student research program?

A parent's checklist before paying for a research program, summer program or résumé booster: what admissions readers say, red flags, and who does the work.

STARC Institute · 2026-11-26 · Follow @Starc_Institute on X

Short answer

Before paying for a student research program, parents should check four things: whether the program selects students or admits anyone who can pay, who the mentor really is, what your student will actually do and own, and what is promised in writing. MIT Admissions and the admissions officers quoted by PBS, CBS and ProPublica give little weight to a program just because it was paid for; what can count is real work your student can explain.

What should parents check before paying for a student research program?

The offers come in many shapes: summer research programs, online mentorships, "publish your first paper" packages. Some are real. Many sell the look of experience. If you're a parent being asked to pay, here is how to tell the difference before the money leaves your account.

We read five public sources that look at this from different sides: MIT Admissions' page on summer programs; a 2015 PBS NewsHour story by Kirk Carapezza of WGBH; a 2020 CBS MoneyWatch article by Megan Cerullo; ProPublica's 2023 investigation of paid research programs by Daniel Golden and Kunal Purohit; and Harvard's Making Caring Common project's tips for parents. They agree on more than you might expect.

Does paying for a program help admissions?

Not because it was paid for. On this point the sources line up.

  • Most programs aren't selective. MIT Admissions says most summer programs "admit all or most students who can pay the (often high) tuition." It adds that competitive-admission programs are often free or comparatively affordable.
  • Admissions offices don't track them. In the PBS story, Brown's dean of admissions said the weight his office gives summer courses is zero, and that it doesn't even know who attended. A former admissions reader in the same story said she didn't place much value on them.
  • A university name on the brochure isn't the university. In the CBS article, a former assistant admissions director at Yale and Georgetown points out that most of these programs aren't run by the admissions or dean's office.

Two of these sources are old (2015 and 2020), so treat their price figures as dated. MIT's current page and the 2023 ProPublica report make the same point.

Where do the sources disagree?

They disagree on whether research experience itself matters. ProPublica reports that research can influence admissions decisions, and that MIT's dean of admissions weighs the mentor's recommendation more than the research. The same article says families typically pay between $2,500 and $10,000 for these services, and that first readers often can't judge a science paper. A former Stanford admissions officer told ProPublica: "They can't tell if a paper in the sciences means anything."

So the honest reading is this. Real work, described by a mentor who saw it, can help. A purchased line on a résumé, or a paper in a journal nobody checks, mostly doesn't. And the work only helps if your student can talk about it.

Where do they go quiet?

None of the five sources gives data on outcomes for students who paid versus those who didn't. None covers college students paying for grad-school or job-market boosters. And none tells parents how to check a mentor's identity. The checklist below fills those gaps with our own advice, and it is only our advice.

The checklist: ask these before you pay

QuestionA good answerA red flag
How are students selected?An application, with some students turned downAnyone who pays is in
Who is the mentor, by name?A name you can find on a university or lab page, with matching photo and field"Faculty from top universities", no names until after payment
How many students per mentor, and how many hours of contact?A specific number, in writing"Flexible" or "as needed"
What will my student do and own?One concrete task, with the student doing the work"We handle the writing" or "the team prepares the paper"
What is promised in writing?A process and a milestone, never an outcomeAcceptance by a journal or college, a letter, a prize
Where did past students' work end up?Named venues you can check yourselfScreenshots, "partner journals"
Is there a selective alternative?You've looked at free or need-based programs firstNo time to look; "seats are filling"
What happens if it doesn't work out?A written cancellation and refund policy you've readVerbal promises only

Three of these deserve a closer look.

The mentor. ProPublica found one program listing mentors as PhDs when they were PhD students. Search the name yourself. A real academic has a page you can find without the program's help.

The writing. ProPublica describes a consultant who said a paper she had heavily revised helped a client get in. If someone else writes it, your student owns a risk, not an achievement. An interviewer can ask about any figure in the paper.

The promise. A program can promise teaching, feedback and a schedule. It cannot honestly promise what a journal editor or an admissions committee will decide.

What is the parent's role?

Smaller than it feels. Harvard's Making Caring Common project advises parents to keep their role in applications limited and defined, and to let students choose activities they care about rather than ones they think admissions officers will value. It also notes that admissions officers tend to be alert to inauthentic or "trumped up" applications.

In practice, that means your student should read the program's page, ask the questions above and decide. If they can't explain why they want to do this particular project, that's your answer, whatever the program says.

How STARC handles this

STARC is a small nonprofit, and we try to make it easy to run this checklist on us. Our research track opens only when an active researcher has room, starts with a short trial task, and agrees on a milestone up front. Publication is never promised. The OPC cohort admits 12 students, reviews applications with school names removed, and judges students by a live product and weekly build logs. Our How it works page lists what we don't do: no ghost-writing, no guaranteed outcomes, no recommendation letters or authorship for sale. If something there doesn't answer your question, ask us.

The short version

Don't pay for the name on the brochure. Pay attention to who selects, who mentors, who does the work and what's promised. If your student will do real work they can explain, and the program promises only what it controls, it may be worth it. If not, look for a selective program first.

Related questions

Do colleges give extra credit for an expensive summer or research program?

Not for the price. Brown's dean of admissions told PBS in 2015 that summer courses get zero extra weight, and MIT Admissions notes that most summer programs admit nearly anyone who can pay tuition. What can matter is the work itself and a mentor who can describe it.

What is the biggest red flag in a paid research program?

Any promise of an outcome the program doesn't control, such as acceptance by a journal or by a college, plus pressure to pay before you can check who the mentor is.

Should I talk to the program, or should my student?

Your student should. Harvard's Making Caring Common project advises parents to keep their role limited and defined, and the questions in this checklist are ones your student should be able to answer about their own project.

Are selective programs always better than paid ones?

Not always, but they send a clearer signal. MIT Admissions points out that competitive-admission programs are often free or comparatively affordable, so it's worth applying to those before paying for an open-enrollment one.
