Is there a free program for university students to build a real AI product?

Yes, a few, but "free" and "real product" both need checking. Where free options exist, what they usually leave out, and a checklist before you join.

STARC Institute · 2026-10-05

Short answer

Yes. University courses, free online courses and a few tuition-free cohorts let students build a real AI product without paying tuition. But many "free" AI programs are courses that end before anything ships, or funnels into a paid upsell. A program worth joining makes you launch something with a public link, find real users and keep an honest record, and it publishes what it costs and what it keeps.

Where can students build a real AI product for free?

There are three honest places to look, and each has a different catch.

  • Your own university. Some universities now run product courses where the final assignment is an AI application, sometimes judged by investors. A Xinhua report from July 2026 describes courses at Communication University of China, Nanjing University and Wenzhou University where students built and pitched AI products. If your school offers one, it's the cheapest option you have. The catch: it runs on a semester timetable, and a course grade doesn't push you to keep users after the term ends.
  • Free online courses and cloud-platform labs. Plenty of good material is free to audit. The catch: you finish a course, not a product. Nobody checks whether anything went live or whether a stranger used it.
  • Tuition-free cohorts. A small number of programs run a fixed group through building and launching a product, without tuition. The catch: they are small, selective and usually ask for real weekly time.

What's missing from most search results is the distinction between learning about AI products and shipping one. Make that distinction first, then look at price.

What does "free" actually mean?

"Free" covers very different deals. Before joining, work out which one you're looking at.

What the page saysWhat it often meansWhat to check
Free courseFree content, paid certificate or "advanced class" laterIs there anything to buy after the free part?
Free trial campA few free sessions, then a sales pitchDoes the schedule end with a "next step" offer?
Free, with income promisesMarketing for a paid productAre earnings or prizes promised? Walk away.
No tuition, refundable depositYou pay something up front and get it back if you take partAre refund rules written? Is there a public ledger?
Fully fundedSomeone else paysWho, and what do they get in return?

The third row is not hypothetical. In September 2025, Beijing's market regulator published a case against a company running free live-streamed AI courses. Its free sessions gradually turned into a paid-course pitch, its student "income" testimonials were edited or copied images, and its "100% win" lottery had the top prize set to zero odds. The company was fined 500,000 yuan under the Anti-Unfair Competition Law. That's the pattern to watch for: free at the door, a funnel behind it.

What counts as a "real product"?

A useful test: could a stranger use it today without you in the room? In practice, a real product has:

  • a public link that works on a phone
  • one job it does for a specific group of people
  • users who aren't your friends or classmates, with some evidence (screenshots, usage logs, an anonymised list)
  • a record of what happened, including what broke and what didn't work

A slide deck, a demo video or a certificate is not a product. A small, ugly tool that ten strangers used is.

A checklist before you join any free AI product program

  1. Does the program end with something live? Look for a public link as the outcome, not a certificate.
  2. Does it require real users? "Present to the class" is fine; "find people who aren't your friends" is better.
  3. Is everything you'll pay listed in writing? That includes deposits, materials and "optional" add-ons.
  4. Are refund rules specific? "Refundable" should come with exact conditions and a deadline.
  5. Does it sell anything afterwards? Paid communities, advanced classes, mentorship upgrades.
  6. Does it promise outcomes? Income, funding, jobs or admissions. A serious program won't.
  7. Who owns what you build? You should own 100%, with no equity or revenue share taken.
  8. Does it ask for money before admitting you? That's a red flag in almost every case.
  9. Is the time commitment stated? Building something real takes hours every week. Vague answers mean it hasn't been thought through.

If a program passes most of these, the price question mostly answers itself.

How STARC's cohort fits this

STARC's One-Person Company cohort is one tuition-free option, built around this checklist. It's 12 students for 8 weeks, about 10 hours a week, for undergraduate and master's students at universities in mainland China (cohort one). You use AI to build a small product, put it online, find real users and write a build log every week.

The money rules are published on the transparency page: no tuition, and a ¥1,000 (US$150) deposit paid only after admission. It comes back in full within 14 days after Demo Day, even if your product found no users, as long as you took part honestly. Kept deposits pay only running costs, and a ledger is published after each cohort. STARC doesn't sell follow-up courses, doesn't promise results and takes no share of your product.

To be clear about where things stand: cohort one hasn't run yet, so there are no past students or results to point to. Applications for cohort one open soon. The application includes a 48-hour task: build a one-page tool with any AI tool and write up to 300 words on what went wrong. Common questions are on the FAQ.

The short version

Free options exist. Your university course, free online material and a few tuition-free cohorts can all get you there. Choose the one that makes you ship to strangers and write honestly about it, check every rule about money in writing, and be wary of any "free" offer that leads to a sales pitch.

Related questions

Are free AI courses enough to build a real product?

Usually not on their own. Courses teach tools; they rarely make you pick a problem, launch publicly and go find users who aren't your friends. That last part is where most of the learning happens.

Why would a free program ask for a deposit?

Free programs tend to lose many participants halfway. A deposit that comes back in full when you take part is a commitment device, not a fee. Check that the refund rules are written down and that kept deposits are accounted for publicly.

How can I tell if a free program is really a sales funnel?

Ask what it sells after the free part. If there is an "advanced class", a paid community, or income promises, treat the free part as marketing. A program that states in writing it sells no follow-up products is easier to trust.

Do I need to know how to code?

Not for a small web product. AI coding assistants handle most of the typing. You need to be willing to read errors, test carefully and talk to users.
