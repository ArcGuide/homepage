Are there nonprofit research mentorship options for undergraduates?

Where undergraduates can get real research mentorship without buying a program: REUs, campus research offices, labs, and how to judge a "nonprofit" label.

STARC Institute · 2026-10-07

Short answer

Yes, and most of them don't advertise. The biggest are NSF-funded REU summer programs, which pay participants rather than charging them, your own university's undergraduate research office, and joining a professor's lab directly. Small independent nonprofits exist too, but judge any program by its written rules, not by the word "nonprofit".

Where can undergraduates get real research mentorship?

When students search for "nonprofit" or "free" research mentorship, they usually mean: guidance from a real researcher, on a real question, without buying a packaged program. Most of that already exists, and almost all of it sits inside universities and federally funded programs. It just isn't marketed the way paid programs are.

Here are the main routes, roughly in order of how easy they are to reach.

What is an REU, and why is it worth applying?

Research Experiences for Undergraduates (REU) are summer research programs funded by the National Science Foundation and hosted at universities across the country. According to NSF's information for students:

  • Undergraduates who are US citizens, permanent residents or US nationals are eligible to apply.
  • Participants receive stipends, and in many cases help with housing, meals and travel.
  • You apply directly to each REU site. Some sites take applications through NSF's Education and Training Application (ETAP) portal; others use their own process. NSF's directory of REU sites lists them.

The economics are the opposite of a paid program: the program pays you to do research. Sites typically run for about ten weeks over the summer, with a mentor and a defined project. The quality still varies with the mentor, so read about the faculty at each site before you apply.

How do I join a professor's lab as an undergrad?

  1. Choose the person before the topic. Look for faculty who have published recently and have current students. Read two of their recent papers before you write.
  2. Write a short, specific email. One paragraph on what you read and what interested you, one on what you can already do (a course, a tool, a dataset), and a direct ask: "Is there a small piece of your current work an undergraduate could help with?"
  3. Ask about the arrangement. Labs take undergraduates as volunteers, for course credit, or in paid positions, depending on the department. It's a normal question to ask.
  4. Agree on deliverables in the first month. A dataset cleaned, a published result reproduced, an experiment run. Write them down.
  5. Meet on a schedule. Thirty minutes every two weeks is enough if you arrive with results and questions.
  6. Keep a record. Notes, code, failed attempts. That's what you'll talk about in interviews and what a professor can honestly write a letter about.

Does "nonprofit" mean a program is honest?

No. "Nonprofit" describes an organization's tax status and how it's supposed to handle money. It says nothing about whether the mentors are real or the promises are sound. Use the same questions for every program, whatever it calls itself:

  • Who is the mentor, and are they active? You should be able to find their recent work.
  • What will I actually do each week? Reading, experiments, writing, or a "project report" written mostly by someone else?
  • What is promised? A guaranteed paper, authorship on someone else's paper, or a recommendation letter as part of the package are the clearest red flags.
  • What happens if the work doesn't pan out? Real research can fail; a program that can't say so is selling a result, not an experience.
  • Can I talk to a past participant I choose myself?

If a program can't answer these in writing, treat it as a commercial service, whatever the label says.

How STARC's research track fits in

STARC is a small nonprofit, and it runs a research track alongside its main One-Person Company cohort. It's worth being honest about its size: a spot opens only when a mentor genuinely has room for one more person, and most of the time there isn't a match.

You leave a research profile, STARC contacts you only if there's a real match, and there's a short trial task so both sides can see whether it works. The short version

Real research mentorship for undergraduates mostly already exists: your school's research office, professors' labs, and NSF-funded REU programs that pay you to take part. None of it is heavily marketed, so you have to go and ask. Choose an active mentor, take on a real piece of their work, agree on deliverables and keep an honest record. For any outside program, nonprofit or not, check who mentors you, what you'll do and what's promised before you join.

Related questions

Is an REU only for students at research universities?

No. REU sites recruit nationally, and many explicitly look for students whose own campus has limited research opportunities. Eligibility for NSF-funded positions requires US citizenship, permanent residency or US national status, and individual sites may add requirements.

Does "nonprofit" mean a research program is honest?

No. A nonprofit label describes how an organization is supposed to handle money, not whether its mentors are real or what it promises you. Ask who the mentor is, what you'll actually do each week, and what's promised, and expect written answers.

My school has almost no research openings. What can I do?

Apply to REUs, which are designed partly for you. Meanwhile, extend a strong course project with the instructor who taught it, and email faculty at nearby universities whose recent papers you've actually read.

Should I pay for a research program if free options are hard to get?

Paying isn't automatically wrong, but be precise about what you're buying. Programs that promise a publication, sell authorship or can't name an active mentor are selling the appearance of research.
