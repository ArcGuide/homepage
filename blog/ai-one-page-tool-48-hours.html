What can I build with AI in 48 hours? One-page tool ideas for beginners

What a beginner can really build with AI in 48 hours: one-page tool ideas, a weekend schedule, and what to leave out so you actually ship.

STARC Institute · 2026-10-30 · Follow @Starc_Institute on X

Short answer

In 48 hours, a beginner can build and publish a one-page web tool that does one job for one kind of person: paste something in, get something useful out. Skip logins, databases and payments, pick a problem you or someone near you actually has, and spend the last few hours deploying it and writing down what broke.

What can you build with AI in 48 hours?

More than you'd guess, and less than you'd hope. The realistic answer for a beginner is a one-page tool: a single web page that takes some input, does one thing to it, and shows a result. A dilution calculator for your chem lab. A page that turns pasted course times into a weekly grid and flags conflicts. A flashcard drill that runs on terms you paste in.

What you probably can't finish in 48 hours, at least not well, is anything with accounts, a shared database, payments or a mobile app store listing. Those aren't impossible. They just eat the weekend before you've shown the tool to a single person.

Why one page? What experienced builders agree on

The people who build small tools with AI most often keep giving the same advice, from different directions.

Keep it to one file. Simon Willison, a software developer who writes widely about AI tools, has built more than 150 single-file "HTML tools" over about two years, almost all written by AI models. His patterns: HTML, CSS and JavaScript in one file, no framework that needs a build step, dependencies loaded from a CDN, and a few hundred lines at most. Small enough that if it breaks, you can have the AI rewrite it in minutes.

Cut the scope until it fits the time you have. Andrew Ng, writing in DeepLearning.AI's newsletter The Batch, describes building a public-speaking practice tool in a couple of spare hours on a Saturday. He got there by cutting: one simulated audience member instead of a crowd, a person behind the scenes choosing reactions instead of AI, a simple 2D avatar. His rule is to shrink the idea until you can build something in the time you actually have.

Start from a problem someone actually has. Paul Graham's essay How to Get Startup Ideas is about companies, not weekend projects, but its test carries over: can you name who wants this right now? He warns against "made-up" ideas that sound plausible but that nobody would use, and suggests you "live in the future and build what seems interesting."

Put it somewhere public. Willison hosts his tools on GitHub Pages. GitHub's quickstart walks through it in a few steps, and notes that changes can take up to 10 minutes to publish.

Where the advice runs out

These sources mostly talk past one another on one point. Willison builds tools for himself, Ng builds a rough prototype to get early feedback, and Graham is talking about ideas worth years of your life. None of them says what a beginner should do when their only deadline is 48 hours and someone else will look at the result.

They're also quiet on two things that matter for a first build:

  • Showing it to a real person. A tool nobody has opened except you is untested, however clean the code.
  • Writing down what went wrong. Ng mentions that feedback on his crude prototype changed his idea, but none of them treats the record of what broke as part of the work. For a student, that record is often what makes the project believable later.

That's where the practical answer below fills in.

One-page tool ideas for beginners

Each of these needs no login, no server and no database. Swap in your own campus, course or club.

Two warnings that save weekends:

  • Don't put an AI API key in a public page. Anyone can open the page source and copy it. Tools that work without a key are the safer first build.
  • Don't collect other people's personal data. No forms asking for names, emails or student IDs. If the tool needs to remember something, keep it in the user's own browser.

A 48-hour plan that ends with something live

  1. Hour 0–2: pick the problem. Write one sentence: "[Who] needs to [do what] and currently [does it badly how]." If you can't name a real person, pick another idea.
  2. Hour 2–3: describe it to the AI. Ask an AI chat tool or coding assistant for a single HTML file that does the one job. Say what goes in and what should come out. Ask for no frameworks.
  3. Hour 3–12: make it work. Test it with real inputs, including messy ones. When it's wrong, paste the error or describe exactly what happened.
  4. Hour 12–20: deploy it. Put it on a public link now, not on Sunday night. Open it on your phone.
  5. Hour 20–40: show it to two or three people who have the problem. Watch them use it without explaining. Fix the one thing that confused them most.
  6. Hour 40–48: write it up. What it does, who it's for, what went wrong and how you fixed it. Keep the AI conversation or commit history as evidence.

If you're behind at hour 20, cut a feature, not the deploy.

The short version

In 48 hours, build one page that does one job for one kind of person. Skip accounts, databases, payments and API keys in the page. Deploy early, show it to someone who has the problem, and write down what broke. That's a real first project.

Related questions

Do I need to know how to code to build a one-page tool with AI?

No. You describe what you want to an AI chat tool or coding assistant, and it writes the HTML, CSS and JavaScript. You do need to test it yourself, notice when it's wrong and ask for fixes.

Where do I put the tool so other people can open it?

A static host such as GitHub Pages works for a single HTML file. GitHub's own quickstart notes that changes can take up to 10 minutes to go live, so don't leave deploying to the last minute.

Can my 48-hour tool call an AI model like ChatGPT inside the page?

Be careful. If you paste an API key into a public page, anyone can read it and use it. For a first 48-hour build, prefer a tool that works without a key, or keep the AI part on your side while you build.

What if my tool is too simple to impress anyone?

Simple is the point. A small tool that works, that someone uses, and that comes with an honest note about what went wrong says more than a big unfinished one.
