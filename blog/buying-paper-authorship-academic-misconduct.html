Is buying authorship on a paper academic misconduct? The risk for college and grad school applicants

Buying authorship breaks journal and university rules, even where it isn't called misconduct. Here's the real risk for applicants.

STARC Institute · 2026-11-06 · Follow @Starc_Institute on X

Short answer

Buying authorship on a paper breaks journal authorship standards and is treated as dishonest by university policy, even though the narrow term "research misconduct" usually means fabrication, falsification or plagiarism. For college and grad school applicants, the larger risk is separate. A credit you didn't earn may count as a false statement on your application, and at schools like Dartmouth that can cost you an offer, your credits or your degree.

Is buying authorship on a paper academic misconduct?

An ad offers a co-author slot on a finished paper. Or a program says your name will appear on a publication by the end of the summer. You'd like to know how serious this is before you pay, and many pages on the subject come from companies selling a rival program.

So we read the primary sources instead: the main journal authorship standard, a current university authorship policy, a university's rule on false application materials, and two pieces of reporting. Here's the honest summary. The sources agree that bought authorship is dishonest. They don't agree on the word "misconduct." And none of them tells you how often applicants get caught.

What makes someone an author?

A widely used standard comes from the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors. It was written for medical journals, and other fields set their own norms, but the logic travels well. An author must meet all four of these:

  1. A substantial contribution to the design of the work, or to getting or analyzing the data.
  2. Drafting the paper or critically revising it.
  3. Approving the final version.
  4. Agreeing to be accountable for all of it.

Funding alone doesn't qualify, and neither does general supervision. Anyone who falls short should be thanked in the acknowledgments, not listed as an author.

The fourth test is the one a purchase fails most clearly. If a question comes up about the data, a bought name can't answer it.

Is it "misconduct"? The sources use the word differently

This is where a quick answer goes wrong.

Florida International University's authorship policy, revised in September 2026, reserves "research misconduct" for fabrication, falsification and plagiarism. It says outright that deciding authorship isn't a research misconduct matter. Read alone, that sounds like a loophole.

It isn't. The same policy lists honorary authorship as a violation with its own process, and says the practice "represents dishonesty to the scientific community." It also quotes a publishing-ethics paper that calls crediting people with little or no contribution an obvious case of misconduct, in the everyday sense.

Two limits apply. The policy doesn't mention purchases by name, so treating a bought slot as honorary authorship is our reading. And it excludes work done only by students as part of coursework, so it wouldn't cover every case. Your own school's policy is the one that counts.

The practical answer: don't rest anything on the label. Whether or not your school files it under "misconduct," no source we read treats a bought name as acceptable.

What's actually for sale?

Two very different things get blurred together.

Authorship slots. An article in The Conversation describes paper mills that sell a place on a paper, from about $197 to $5,472, sometimes with a promise that it will be published. It notes that no well-run journal can make that promise. This is the clear case.

Paid research programs. ProPublica's 2023 investigation covered online services that pair high schoolers with paid mentors for 10 to 15 weeks, with most families paying $2,500 to $10,000. That isn't the same as buying a name. Students do write papers, and some of the work ProPublica described was serious. But it also reported a former consultant who said she heavily revised a client's paper, and an editor who wondered how much of the work was the mentor's.

So the line isn't money. It's whether the contribution behind your name is really yours.

What's the risk for applicants?

Here's what the sources support, stage by stage.

When it surfacesWhat can happenBasis
While you're being readProbably little at first; a first reader may spend 10 minutes or less and just note the paperProPublica

Three honest caveats. Dartmouth's rule covers "a false statement or falsified material" and doesn't mention authorship, so applying it here is our reading. It's one undergraduate policy, and graduate programs set their own rules. And we found no published case of a student losing an offer over bought authorship, so we can't tell you the odds.

We also won't tell you it's safe because a busy reader might miss it. ProPublica's account cuts both ways: a weak paper may slip past a first reader, and the same paper may not do much for you if nobody can tell what it's worth. You'd be taking on a long-lasting risk for a line that may carry little weight. And a credit you can't explain is hard to defend in any interview, which we cover in our post on projects you can't claim.

A checklist before you pay or sign

This list is our own advice, built from the sources above.

  1. Ask what you'll personally do. If the paper already exists, or the answer is vague, stop.
  2. Run the four tests. Could you meet all four, including answering for the data?
  3. Be wary of any promise of publication. Journals decide that, not sellers. Our post on whether paid research internships are worth it has more questions to ask.
  4. Ask who else is on the paper and why. Unknown co-authors from unrelated fields are a warning sign.
  5. Read your school's authorship policy. Look on your research office's site.
  6. Practice the five-minute test. Could you explain the question, method and weakest result with no notes?
  7. Describe your role precisely. "Ran the survey analysis" beats "co-author." Claim only what you did.

If you're already on a paper you barely touched, leave it off your application until you've sorted it out. Talk to the corresponding author and ask your campus research integrity office. Fixing it now is far easier than explaining it later.

The short version

Buying authorship is dishonest under every standard we read, whatever label your school uses. The sharper risk for applicants is misrepresentation, which can follow you past admission. Before you claim a credit, ask one question: could you answer for all of it?

Related questions

Is paying for a research mentor the same as buying authorship?

No. Paying someone to teach you while you do the work is a different thing from paying to have your name added to work you didn't do. The test is your contribution, not whether money changed hands.

Can a college find out that I bought authorship?

We found no published data on how often this is caught. A first reader may only note that a paper exists, but admissions staff can also ask mentors about you, and a person who can't explain the work is exposed in any interview.

What if I'm already listed on a paper I barely worked on?

Don't put it on an application as it stands. Talk to the corresponding author about what you really did, ask whether the credit should become an acknowledgment, and ask your campus research integrity office for advice.

Is paying a journal's publication fee the same as buying authorship?

No. Many online journals charge authors a fee to publish. That pays for publishing a paper, not for a name on it. It's still worth checking the journal's standards before you submit.
