Build your own product, join a startup team, or take an internship: which should a college student choose?

Your own product, a classmate's startup or an internship? Pick by what you need next, then check the risks each option hides before you commit.

STARC Institute · 2026-11-27 · Follow @Starc_Institute on X

Short answer

Choose by what you need next. If you want a job offer after graduation, an internship is the most direct path, because many employers hire from their intern pool. If you want broad responsibility, a startup team teaches fast but carries more risk. If you want proof that you can finish something on your own, build your own small product. Many students do one as the main line and one on the side.

Your own product, a startup team or an internship: which should a college student choose?

You have three offers on the table, or at least three ideas. A recruiter is pushing summer internship applications. A friend is starting a company and wants you on the team. And with today's AI coding tools, you could build something yourself. All three are real options. They teach different things and carry different risks.

No single page compares all three. What exists is advice from career centers, a hiring survey, a professor who champions startups and a law firm's guide for founders. Read together, they point to a fairly clear way to choose. Where we go beyond them, we say so.

Where the sources agree

Internships are the most direct road to a job offer. The National Association of Colleges and Employers reported in April 2026 that the intern conversion rate hit its highest mark in five years: 63.1% for 2024-25 interns, from a survey of 284 employer organizations. The article calls internships a path to a first career position. If a full-time offer is what you need next, that number matters more than any opinion.

Startups teach breadth, at a price. CU Boulder's career services, in a post on the benefits and challenges of working for a startup, lists the upside: you can influence outcomes more, and you may cover many parts of the business in one role. It names the downside just as plainly: frequent change and a higher risk of failure. Its own summary: "Startups are amazing places to work. But, they aren't for everyone."

Your own projects count, if you're specific. An Ohio State engineering career services post by a peer career coach argues that projects are among the strongest resume items early in college, because they show applied skill instead of a list of tools. It asks you to state your individual contribution, and it treats projects as a complement to internships and research, not a substitute.

Where they disagree, or go quiet

On risk, they pull in different directions. Professor Danny Warshay told an interviewer from Intern From Home (republished by Augsburg University's career center) that students who want to start something shouldn't drift into finance and consulting by default. He thinks joining an early startup is a good way to learn entrepreneurship, and that it is cheaper to make mistakes inside someone else's company than in your own. He calls his own view biased. CU Boulder's caution about instability is the counterweight.

They are quiet on three things. None compares building your own product with joining a classmate's team. None says how recruiters weigh a product you built alone against an internship. And none of these posts discusses what AI coding tools have changed: one student can now ship a working product without a team. The decision table below fills those gaps with our own reasoning, not with data.

A decision rule: pick by your next step

What you need nextMain lineSide line
A full-time offer at a larger companyInternship (apply early in the recruiting season)A small product during the semester
To learn how companies are builtJoin an early startup team, with your role in writingKeep one class project or product you fully own
Proof you can finish something aloneYour own small product with real usersInternship applications for next summer
You might found a company laterBuild alone first to test an idea cheaply, then decide on a teamAn internship or startup job to learn from others' mistakes

Why one main line? Because each option rewards depth. An intern who does useful work gets a return offer; a half-present one doesn't. A product with ten real users says more than three abandoned prototypes.

What each option hides: a checklist

Before you take an internship: 1. Ask what interns actually do, and who they work with each week. 2. Ask how many interns from last year received offers. It's a fair question. 3. Check that you'll have something concrete to describe in your next interview.

Before you join a classmate's startup: 1. Write down your role and weekly hours, and agree on them. 2. If equity is involved, discuss the split early and put it in writing. WilmerHale's founders guide warns that a split that doesn't match contributions can cause lasting ill will, and it recommends vesting on founders' shares. 3. Settle who owns the code and design. The same guide notes the company has to own or license its intellectual property, and that this doesn't happen automatically. 4. If you're an international student, ask your international student office first. The guide says a foreign student may own equity but needs authorization before doing work for a company. 5. Ask what happens when someone graduates or leaves, and write that down too.

Before you build your own product: 1. Pick a problem you can test with real people within a few weeks. 2. Ship a small version early, at a public link. 3. Keep a weekly log of what shipped, what the numbers were and what broke. It becomes your interview material. 4. Decide in advance what "it didn't work" looks like, so you stop on purpose rather than drift.

What does each one prove?

An internship proves that an organization trusted you with real work and that you could work inside its structure. A startup role proves that you can handle ambiguity and wear several hats. Your own product proves that you can find a problem, finish something and deal with users who owe you nothing. None replaces the others. When you describe any of them, say what you personally did, as the Ohio State post advises for projects.

The short version

Need an offer? Intern. Want to learn how companies are built? Join a startup, with your role and any equity in writing. Want proof you can finish alone? Build your own product and log it. Pick one main line, keep one small side line, and describe exactly what you did.

Related questions

Does an internship lead to a full-time job?

Often. NACE's 2026 internship survey of 284 employers reported a 63.1% intern conversion rate for 2024-25 interns, the highest in five years. It is a path, not a promise.

What should I check before joining a classmate's startup?

Get the basics in writing: your role, any equity split and how it vests, and who owns the code. A law-firm founders guide notes that a company does not own its intellectual property automatically. International students should also ask their international student office before doing any work.

Does a project I built myself count on a resume?

Yes, if you say exactly what you did. An Ohio State engineering career post calls well-done projects some of the strongest resume items early in college. It treats them as a complement to internships, not a replacement.

Can I do more than one?

Usually one main line and one small side line works better than three half-efforts. A summer internship plus a small product during the semester is a common, workable split.
