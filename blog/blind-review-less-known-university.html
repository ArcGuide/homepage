Do students from less well-known universities stand a chance in a blind-reviewed program?

What a blind review removes, what it can't, and how a student from a less well-known university can build an application that wins on the material itself.

STARC Institute · 2026-10-09

Short answer

Yes, if the review is truly blind and the application asks for things any student can produce. A blind review hides the school name, so what's left to judge is the work you submit. It does not erase every advantage, so the best strategy is to send something a reviewer can open and check, such as a live project and an honest account of how you built it.

Do students from less well-known universities stand a chance in a blind-reviewed program?

Most selective programs say they "look at the whole person". In practice the school name is the first thing a reader sees, and it is a fast shortcut. If you study at a less well-known university, you have probably felt that shortcut work against you before anyone read the rest.

A blind review removes that shortcut on purpose. The reviewer scores the application without the school name. That changes the question from "where are you from?" to "what did you send?" For a student whose work is better than their school's reputation, that is a real change.

But blind review is a method, not a promise. It helps you only if two things are true: the blinding is done properly, and the application asks for material that any student can produce without money or connections.

What does a blind review actually hide, and what can't it hide?

A blind review usually removes the school name, and sometimes the major, city or other identifying details, before scoring. What it can't do is hide everything that hints at a background.

There is evidence that blinding is harder than it sounds. A 2000 study in the BMJ tried anonymising more than 2,000 medical school application forms. The authors found that names appeared up to 15 times on some forms, that a reviewer could still identify many applicants from details in their personal statements, and that the anonymised scoring did not raise the scores of the group it was meant to help. They concluded that the approach, as they ran it, could not be recommended.

That study was about personal names, not school names, and about a long written form. The lesson still carries over: blinding works best when the material being judged is concrete and checkable, and worst when it is a long narrative full of context clues.

So a blind review is strongest when it judges work, not description.

How to tell whether a program's "we don't look at your school" is real

Ask these before you spend time on an application:

QuestionA real answer looks likeA weak answer looks like
What exactly is hidden at review?"School names are removed before anyone scores.""We value diversity."
Who scores applications?Named roles, such as the founders or the mentors"Our team" with no detail
What are the scoring criteria?Two to four specific criteria, published"Overall fit"
What do you ask applicants to submit?Work anyone can produce: a task, a projectTranscripts, awards, ranking, recommendation letters
Does AI score applicants?A clear yes or no, and what AI is used forNo answer
Does everyone hear back?Yes, with a decision either wayOnly admitted students are contacted
Do you charge before admission?NoAn "application fee" or "assessment fee"

The fourth row matters most. If a program hides your school name but scores you on awards, internships and recommendation letters, those things still track school resources closely. A blind review only levels the field when the thing being judged is something you can make yourself.

What to put in an application when your school name won't help you

If the school name is hidden, the material does all the work. Here is a practical way to build it:

  1. Make one small thing that works. A one-page web tool, a script that solves a real annoyance, a small data analysis with the code and data public. Small is fine. It has to open.
  2. Write down what went wrong. A short, honest note on the bugs, the dead ends and what you changed. Reviewers who read many applications can tell a real account from a polished one.
  3. Pick a real problem for a real person. "A tool for students to plan group projects" is vague. "My roommate spends 20 minutes every week merging three class timetables, so I built a page that does it" is concrete.
  4. Cut the institution out of the story. Write about what you did, not where you did it. This helps the blinding work and keeps your text focused.
  5. Don't pad. Ten activities listed in one line each say less than one project explained well.
  6. Check your links on another device. A broken link in a blind review is a zero, because the reviewer has nothing else to go on.

None of this needs money, a famous lab or an internship. That is the point.

Where blind review doesn't help, and what to do about it

Be realistic about three limits.

  • Time is not equal. Students who work part-time or commute have fewer hours. A program that needs about ten hours a week is a real cost, so check that before applying.
  • Some background still shows. Your writing, your tools and your examples carry some trace of your environment. That's fine. A reviewer judging work is still a better deal than one judging a name.
  • Places are limited. A fair process can still say no. If it does, the thing you built for the application is still yours and still useful elsewhere: a portfolio piece, a talking point in an interview, a start on something bigger.

Blind review raises your odds by removing a shortcut. It doesn't change how many places there are.

How STARC's review works

STARC is a small nonprofit, and its one-person company cohort uses a blind review. From the published rules:

  • School names are removed before review. Any major is welcome, and you don't need to know how to code.
  • You apply with something you made, plus a 48-hour task: use any AI tool to build a one-page tool anyone can open, with a note of up to 300 words on what went wrong and how you fixed it. Rough is fine.
  • Three criteria: did you ship, is your account honest, and is it a real problem for real people.
  • A person reads every application. AI only organizes the materials; it doesn't score anyone.
  • 12 admitted, 3 on a waitlist, and everyone hears back either way.
  • No money before admission. No tuition; admitted students pay a refundable deposit.

The first cohort hasn't run yet, so there are no past results to point to. The full process and rules are on the transparency page, and the separate research track opens only when a mentor genuinely has room.

The short version

A blind review gives students from less well-known universities a real chance, but only when the blinding is real and the application judges work you can make yourself. Ask what's hidden, who scores, and what they score. Then send one small thing that works and an honest note on how you built it. That is the part no school name can add or take away.

Related questions

Can a reviewer still guess my school in a blind review?

Sometimes. Email addresses, course names, club names and local details can give it away. A well-run blind review strips the obvious fields, and you can help by keeping your material about the work rather than the institution.

Is blind review the same as "we don't care about background"?

Not quite. It means the school name is hidden at the scoring stage. Ask what else is hidden, who does the scoring, and what the scoring criteria are. A program that can't answer those is using the phrase as a slogan.

What should I put in an application if I have no awards or internships?

Something you made, even if it's small and rough, plus an honest note on what went wrong. A live link to a one-page tool that works says more than a list of activities, because a reviewer can check it in a minute.

Does a blind-reviewed program guarantee a fair result?

No. It removes one strong signal, the school name, so the remaining material carries more weight. Reviewers still make judgment calls, and places are limited.
