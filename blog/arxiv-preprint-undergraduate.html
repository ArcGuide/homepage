Does posting a preprint on arXiv help an undergraduate's grad school application?

When an arXiv preprint helps an undergrad's grad school application, what arXiv's 2026 endorsement rules mean for students, and how to list it.

STARC Institute · 2026-11-20 · Follow @Starc_Institute on X

Short answer

Posting a preprint on arXiv can help an undergraduate's grad school application when the work is real, your role is clear and a researcher who supervised it will vouch for it, because readers can open and judge the paper itself. It does not help as a label. arXiv says its screening is not peer review, and since January 2026 most first-time submitters need a personal endorsement from an established author in the field.

Does posting a preprint on arXiv help an undergraduate's grad school application?

The short version: the preprint itself is not the credential. What it does is make your research readable. A line that says "manuscript in preparation" asks the committee to trust you. A link asks them to judge the work. That is good news if the work is solid and bad news if it isn't.

We read four sources on this: arXiv's own policies (its endorsement page, its moderation page and its January 2026 endorsement update); Matt Might's long-running guide to getting into grad school in science and computer science; an ASBMB Today essay by Ken Hallenbeck (2022) arguing that preprints help early careers; and the University of Pittsburgh career center's guide to listing publications on a resume.

What the sources agree on

Readers judge the work, not the label. Might's guide says admissions looks first at prior research, "published or unpublished," and that if you send unpublished work, someone from the field will judge its quality. Hallenbeck makes the same point from the other side: work listed as "in preparation" or "under review" can't be examined by a hiring committee, while a preprint lets you link the manuscript directly on your CV. Put together, a preprint's value is that it turns a claim into something a reader can check.

A preprint is not peer review. arXiv is direct about this. Its moderation page says the process "is not a peer-review process," and moderators don't give feedback. Its endorsement page says endorsement is not peer review either, and endorsers don't have to verify that the work is correct. So a preprint tells a reader that a paper exists and passed a basic screen. It does not tell them the result is right.

Your name means you own every word. arXiv's moderation policy says each author takes full responsibility for all of a paper's contents, however they were generated. Tools can't be listed as authors. For a student, that means you must be able to defend every section in an interview, including the parts a coauthor wrote.

What changed in 2026, and why it matters for undergrads

For years, a university email address was often enough to get automatically endorsed on arXiv. That ended on January 21, 2026. Under the updated policy, automatic endorsement now needs two things at once: an institutional email address and previous authorship on an accepted paper in that subject area. Anyone else, which describes most undergraduates, needs a personal endorsement from an established arXiv author in the same area. arXiv staff can't endorse anyone or waive the rule, and the post notes that "endorsement is not a guarantee." arXiv gives its reason: a sharp rise in non-scientific submissions.

Since October 1, 2026, arXiv also limits each submitter to two new submissions per calendar month, with at most three active at a time.

The practical effect is simple. If you did the work with a researcher who already posts in that area, they can endorse you or submit for you. If nobody established is willing to put their name next to yours, that is useful information about the paper.

Where the sources disagree, or say nothing

Might's one-word advice is "PUBLISH." He also says you can still get in without publications; it's just harder. His guide is written for science, engineering, math and computer science, where preprints are common. Norms differ in other fields, and in some biology and social science areas a preprint is far less routine.

Hallenbeck's essay is about job candidates, not undergraduate applicants, and he discloses that he took part in the ASAPbio Fellows program in 2021. The Pittsburgh guide covers how to list published, in-press, submitted and in-progress work, but doesn't mention preprints at all.

None of the four sources says how admissions committees score an unreviewed undergraduate preprint against, say, a strong letter alone. We found no data on that. Anyone who tells you a preprint adds a fixed amount to your chances is guessing.

A checklist before you post

This is our own synthesis of the sources above.

  1. Your supervisor agrees it's ready. If your mentor wouldn't show it to a colleague, don't show it to a committee.
  2. Every coauthor agrees to post. Hallenbeck lists this among the basic steps.
  3. The target journal or conference allows preprints. Check its policy before you post, and pick a license.
  4. You have an endorser, or a coauthor who can submit. Since January 2026, assume you'll need one.
  5. You can explain every section. Methods, every figure, every limitation, including what AI tools did.
  6. Your role is stated somewhere a reader will see it. In your statement of purpose and, ideally, in your supervisor's letter.

How to list it

The Pittsburgh guide suggests that students with limited experience put research under a "Relevant Experience" or "Academic Experience" section, and only create a separate "Publications" section when the work is extensive. For a preprint, we suggest the full author list in order, the exact title, "arXiv preprint" with its identifier, and the month and year posted. Don't name a journal it hasn't been accepted to. Update the entry if it's later accepted.

The short version

A preprint helps when it makes good work visible and someone who supervised it will vouch for you. It doesn't help as a badge. Post when the paper is ready to be read.

Related questions

Can an undergraduate post a paper on arXiv alone?

Usually not without help. Since January 21, 2026, automatic endorsement requires both an institutional email and prior authorship of an accepted paper in that subject area. Everyone else needs a personal endorsement from an established arXiv author in the same area, and arXiv staff can't provide one.

Is an arXiv preprint a publication?

It is public and citable, but it is not peer reviewed. arXiv says plainly that its moderation process is not peer review, so list it as a preprint, not as a publication.

Will a preprint get me into a PhD program?

No single item does that. A computer science professor's widely read admissions guide says publications are the strongest signal and that you can get in without them; it's just harder. A preprint helps when the work holds up and a letter from your supervisor explains your part in it.

Should I post a preprint just to have something on my CV?

No. Moderators can decline work that lacks originality or significance, and an admissions reader who opens a thin paper learns something you didn't want them to learn. Post when the work is ready to be read, not when the deadline is near.
