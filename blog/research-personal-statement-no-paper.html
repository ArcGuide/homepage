How do I write about research in a personal statement with no publication?

How to write about research in a personal statement or statement of purpose when you have no paper, unfinished work or a null result, with a paragraph template.

STARC Institute · 2026-11-17 · Follow @Starc_Institute on X

Short answer

To write about research in a personal statement with no publication, describe the work itself: the question, why it mattered, what you personally did, what happened (including a null or unfinished result) and what you learned. How do I write about research in a personal statement with no publication?

Most undergraduates who apply to graduate school with research experience don't have a paper. Projects run longer than a semester, experiments fail, and the student graduates before the work is finished. So the real question isn't whether you can still write about it. It's how to make the work legible to a reader who has never seen it.

We read four pieces of advice written by people who read these statements or teach students to write them: Mor Harchol-Balter's guide to applying to PhD programs from Carnegie Mellon (last updated 2014), a 2023 Society of Physics Students article in which Kendra Redmond interviews Cornell physics professor Ritchie Patterson, the MIT AeroAstro Communication Lab's statement of purpose guide, and Purdue College of Science's guide to personal statements for research. They agree on more than you might expect.

What the sources agree on: the paper is not the point

Research experience doesn't require a publication, or even a result. Harchol-Balter is direct about this. Prior research experience, she writes, doesn't mean you published, and doesn't even mean the work produced a result, since results can take years. What faculty need is confidence that you know what doing research is like.

Readers want the problem, your approach, and what you learned. Harchol-Balter's suggested research paragraph asks four questions: what problem were you trying to solve, why did it matter, what approaches did you try, what did you learn. She adds: "It's fine to say that you were unable to fully solve your problem." The Society of Physics Students piece, built on Patterson's advice, lists nearly the same things: the big-picture goal, the specific tasks you did, the obstacles you hit and how you handled them, and the outcome tied back to the goal.

Null and unfinished results are legitimate outcomes. One example of an outcome in the Society of Physics Students article is showing that an effect was too small to matter. Its line "Science never works the first time" may be the most useful sentence a student without a paper can read. The MIT guide says the same thing in a different way: without publications, focus on how you approach problems and what you learned, because "A research experience is still a learning experience."

Be honest about where you are. MIT's guide asks applicants to frame their current knowledge level honestly and name the techniques they still want to learn. Purdue's guide asks you to make yourself as appealing as possible "while being honest about yourself."

Where they differ, and where they're silent

The sources pull in slightly different directions on tone. Purdue advises positive statements, saying what experience you have instead of what you don't. Harchol-Balter says it's fine to admit you didn't fully solve the problem. These fit together: state limits as facts about the work, not as apologies about yourself. "The model didn't beat the baseline, and here is why I think so" is a finding. "Unfortunately, I don't have a publication" is an apology.

They're also narrow. Harchol-Balter writes from the view of top computer science PhD programs, and her guide is from 2014. The Society of Physics Students piece is about physics. None of the four says how much a paper is worth compared with a strong letter and a clear account of the work, and none of them cover master's programs specifically. Our reading is that the same logic holds: the reader is judging whether you can do research, and the paper is only one piece of evidence for that. Treat that as our view, not a finding.

One more point from Harchol-Balter is easy to miss. At top programs, she writes, the most important part of the application is your prior research and what your letters and statement say about it. Your statement and your mentor's letter describe the same project. They should match.

A paragraph template for research with no paper

Use one or two paragraphs per project, as the Society of Physics Students article suggests. Each sentence below answers one of the questions the sources agree on.

  1. The question, in one sentence. "I tested whether a simpler model could predict X as well as the published method, using a public data set."
  2. Why it mattered. One sentence on what changes if the answer is yes or no.
  3. What you did, in your own verbs. Cleaned the data, wrote the pipeline, ran the comparison, designed the control. Separate your part from what the lab or mentor supplied.
  4. One obstacle and your decision. What went wrong, and what you chose to do about it. This is where readers see how you think.
  5. What happened, stated plainly. Including "the effect was too small to detect at this sample size" or "the work is ongoing; I finished the first set of experiments."
  6. What you learned or would do next. A specific next step, not "this taught me perseverance."
  7. The link forward. How this connects to what you want to study in the program you're applying to.

Before you submit, send the research paragraph to your mentor and ask one question: does this match what you'd write about me? If your statement and their letter disagree, the reader will notice.

The short version

A paper is one kind of evidence, not the only kind. Name the question, show what you did and decided, state the outcome honestly even if it was null, and say what you'd do next. Then make sure your mentor would tell the same story.

Related questions

Should I apologize for not having a paper?

No. Purdue's College of Science career guide advises stating what experience you have rather than what you lack. Say what you did and what state the work is in. Leave the apology out.

Can I write about a project that failed or gave a null result?

Yes. A Society of Physics Students article built on a Cornell physics professor's advice gives showing that an effect was too small to matter as an example of a real outcome, and a Carnegie Mellon guide says it's fine to say you couldn't fully solve your problem. Explain why it didn't work and what you'd try next.

How long should the research part be?

The Society of Physics Students article suggests one to two paragraphs per research project. Pick the one or two projects that best show how you think, not every lab you've touched.

Can I list a paper as "in preparation" to look stronger?

Only if a manuscript is actually being written and your mentor would describe it the same way. Your recommenders will be describing the same work, so any gap between your statement and their letter shows.
