What can a professor honestly write in a recommendation letter after 3 months of research?

After 3 months a professor can vouch for what they saw you do, not for who you'll become. What fits in the letter, what doesn't, and how to prepare.

STARC Institute · 2026-11-25 · Follow @Starc_Institute on X

Short answer

After 3 months of research, a professor can honestly write about what they watched you do, such as the task you owned, how you handled a setback, and how much guidance you needed. They can't honestly claim long-term growth, or rank you against years of students, on that evidence alone. Whether three months is enough depends on hours and contact more than the calendar. One Berkeley lab accepts a summer of about 270 hours, while a Purdue lab typically asks for two semesters.

What can a professor write in a recommendation letter after 3 months of research?

You spent a summer, or a term, on a research project. Now an application wants a letter. You're wondering whether your mentor knows you well enough, and what they could truthfully say.

We read five public sources that approach this from different sides: letter policies from UC Berkeley's Language and Cognitive Development Lab and Purdue's Cognition and Learning Lab, the Goldwater Scholarship's instructions for letter writers, an October 2025 guide by Jack Baker and Nicole Paul (Baker is at Stanford), and an archived, undated Penn State course on the ethics of recommendation letters prepared by Joe Schall. They agree on what makes a letter useful. They disagree on how long is long enough.

Is 3 months enough? It depends on hours and contact

The two lab policies show the spread.

  • The Purdue lab typically writes only for students who have worked there at least two semesters. The reason is plain: with someone who hasn't been there long, the professor "won't have much to say." The policy adds that exceptions for less time are possible.
  • The Berkeley lab asks for two semesters in its research assistant program, or one summer in its internship program, which it puts at about 270 hours.

So one summer can qualify when the hours are there. Do the arithmetic on your own project. Spread over 12 weeks, 270 hours is a little over 22 hours a week. Five hours a week for the same 12 weeks is 60 hours. Both are "3 months of research", and they don't support the same letter.

The Goldwater instructions set no minimum. They ask a research mentor to state how long they have known the student and in what capacity. Your reader will probably see the dates. A strong letter doesn't hide them.

Contact matters as much as hours. The Baker and Paul guide says you need people who have "actually observed you doing substantive work." A mentor who met you on one group video call a month observed very little, however many hours you logged.

What can they vouch for, and what can't they?

The Goldwater list for research mentors is the most concrete we found. It asks about your contribution, independence, role on the team, skills, research traits backed by examples, motivation, and how you compare with others who went on to research careers. Here is our own reading of what three months can support.

Part of the letterAfter 3 months, usually honestUsually not honest yet
RelationshipExact dates, hours, who supervised you"I have known this student well for years"
ContributionThe one task you owned and why it matteredCredit for the whole project
IndependenceHow much guidance you needed, and how that changed"Works as an independent researcher"
SkillsMethods they saw you use or learnSkills listed only on your résumé
SetbacksOne problem you hit and what you did nextA long record of resilience
ComparisonAgainst students they supervised in a similar role, if there are enough"Top 1% of my career", on one summer
Promise"From what I saw, I would work with them again"Firm predictions about your career

Notice how much is in the middle column. Three months is enough for one real scene, and one scene can carry a letter. Baker and Paul contrast generic "excellent" and "hard-working" letters with one describing how a student reworked a research approach after weeks of failed attempts. That story fits inside a summer.

Where do the sources agree, and where do they go quiet?

They agree that evidence beats praise. Baker and Paul say the strongest letters give specific, personal evidence. Goldwater wants each research trait backed by an example, and says letters that only recite grades or interpersonal skills without evidence don't work. The Berkeley lab warns that letters based only on one of its professor's classes would be very general.

They agree, in different words, that some letters shouldn't be written. Penn State's list of good reasons to decline includes knowing nothing beyond a student's grades. If a mentor hesitates, believe them. A polite no protects you from a thin letter.

They differ on drafting. The Penn State page says faculty sometimes invite students to draft their own letters, and that some see this as wholly unethical. Baker and Paul never suggest writing the letter; they tell students to send their materials and concrete accomplishments to discuss.

They go quiet on two things. None gives data on how admissions readers weigh a short-project letter against a long one. And none discusses remote projects, where a mentor sees less by default. For those, our advice is below, and it's only our advice.

How do you make 3 months writable?

  1. Week 1: ask what you'll own. One task with your name on it gives a letter its subject.
  2. Every week: send a short note. What you did, what broke, what you tried, what's next. These notes are what a mentor rereads months later. If your project is remote, they may be most of what your mentor saw.
  3. Every week: ask one real question. How you think is easier to describe when it's on the record.
  4. Mid-project: ask for criticism. Then act on it visibly. Growth is hard to show in three months, unless someone told you what to fix and saw you fix it.
  5. Last week: write a one-page summary. Dates, hours, your part, other people's parts, one setback, links to code or figures.
  6. Then ask the right question: "Do you feel you've seen enough of my work to write a specific letter?" It gives them room to say no.
  7. If yes, send your materials early and name two or three things they actually saw. Check their deadline rules. The Berkeley lab wants four weeks; the Purdue lab wants two months.

If you stay in touch and keep contributing, a short project can become a longer one. That's the simplest way to a stronger letter.

Three warning signs

  • A letter promised before any work is done. The content doesn't exist yet. Someone is offering a signature.
  • A letter signed by someone who never saw your work. Penn State's guide says that when one person drafts and another co-signs, a statement of who wrote what belongs in the letter.
  • A letter that says more than happened. You may be asked about it in an interview, and you'll have to live up to it.

The short version

A good three-month letter is narrow and true. It says when, how much, what you did and what the professor saw. Make sure there's something to see, keep a record of it, and let the letter be exactly that size.

Related questions

Is 3 months long enough to ask a research mentor for a letter?

It can be, if you put in serious hours and the mentor saw your work directly. Published lab policies differ, from a summer of about 270 hours to two semesters, so check your mentor's policy and ask whether they could write a strong, specific letter.

Can I write a draft of the letter myself?

Send facts instead, such as a dated list of what you did, with links. A Penn State guide notes that some faculty see student-drafted letters as unethical, and hopes any student draft is a list of accomplishments rather than the finished letter.

What if a postdoc or PhD student supervised me, not the professor?

The honest letter makes clear who observed you. Ask whether the daily supervisor can contribute and whether the professor will say whose observations the letter rests on. The same Penn State guide says a letter drafted by one person and co-signed by another should state that.

Is a short, detailed letter better than a long, general one?

The sources we read all favor specific evidence over general praise. None of them measures how readers score letters, so treat this as strong advice rather than a statistic.
