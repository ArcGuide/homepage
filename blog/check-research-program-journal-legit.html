How do I check if the journal in a paid research program is legitimate?

A paid research program says your paper will appear in a peer-reviewed journal. Here's how to check that journal or conference yourself, in about 30 minutes.

STARC Institute · 2026-11-10 · Follow @Starc_Institute on X

Short answer

To check if the journal in a paid research program is legitimate, verify its claims from the outside rather than on its own website. Confirm the ISSN, the publisher and any claimed indexing in the databases themselves, ask how peer review works and who the reviewers are, find out whether the program owns or funds the journal, and treat a promise of acceptance or a very fast review as a warning sign. No single check settles it, so use several.

How do you check if the journal in a paid research program is legitimate?

The program brochure says your paper will be "published in a peer-reviewed journal." That sentence is doing a lot of work, and you're the one who'll have to stand behind it on an application.

We read five sources that address this: two university library guides, the cross-industry Think. Check. Submit. checklist, a Federal Trade Commission case and a ProPublica investigation of paid research programs for high schoolers. They agree on the method: check the journal's claims from the outside, not on its own website. They disagree on how much fees and published lists can tell you. And none of them says how admissions readers weigh a student journal.

Why is this a real question, not paranoia?

Two documented cases set the scene.

ProPublica's 2023 investigation found at least 20 online research programs for high schoolers serving at least 12,000 students a year, with most families paying between $2,500 and $10,000. It reported that some services present affiliated publications as independent journals. One student journal was founded and funded by a program and didn't tell readers. Another described itself as peer-reviewed with a large reviewer pool, yet four of the eight reviewers ProPublica contacted said they had never been asked to review.

The second case is older and bigger. In 2019 a federal court sided with the Federal Trade Commission against the publisher OMICS and imposed a $50.1 million judgment. The FTC said many articles were published with little to no peer review, many people presented as editors had not agreed to be affiliated with the journals, the journals were advertised as accepted by a major indexing service that had in fact refused to index them, authors were not properly told about the steep publishing fees they would be charged, and prominent researchers were named as conference presenters when many of them had not agreed to take part.

Neither case means a given program is dishonest. Both show which claims are worth checking.

What do the sources agree on?

All five point the same way: don't trust the journal's description of itself.

  • Identity. The Think. Check. Submit. journal checklist, a cross-industry initiative led by publishing and library groups including COPE, DOAJ and ISSN, asks whether you can identify and contact the publisher, and whether the journal's name and ISSN check out in the ISSN portal.
  • Indexing. The University of Pittsburgh library guide tells students to look the journal up in the Directory of Open Access Journals and to look the publisher up in member lists such as COPE and OASPA. The OMICS case shows why: an indexing claim on a journal's site can simply be false.
  • Peer review. Think. Check. Submit. asks whether the journal is clear about the type of peer review it uses, and asks that it not promise acceptance or an unusually short review time. In the Pittsburgh guide's case study, one of the signs is a journal that never makes clear whether it uses peer review or what kind, promising only that publication is fast and easy.
  • People. The FTC case turned partly on editors and conference presenters who never agreed to be listed. A name on a website proves nothing until the person's own page confirms it.

Where do they disagree, or go quiet?

On fees. Think. Check. Submit. only asks that fees be stated clearly, and many legitimate open access journals charge authors. Baruch College's student publishing guide agrees that charging a fee doesn't make a journal predatory, but suggests students look first at journals that don't charge. So a fee alone isn't a verdict. A fee plus fast acceptance plus vague review is a pattern.

On lists. The Pittsburgh guide leans on directories and member lists. The Baruch guide, citing a 2019 analysis by Grudniewicz and colleagues, notes that assessments of which journals are predatory don't tally and that the same title can appear on both sides; it also says no single criterion settles it. Use lists as evidence, not as a verdict.

On admissions. This is where every source goes quiet. ProPublica reports that admissions staff can confirm a paper was published but are often at a loss to judge its quality, that several of these journals have confusingly similar names, and that a first reader often takes 10 minutes or less per application. Nobody we read says how much a student journal is worth. We won't guess either.

A 30-minute check before you pay or sign

This is our own checklist, built from the sources above. Do it with the program's materials open.

CheckHowWarning sign
Which journal, exactly?Ask for the full name and ISSN in writing"Our partner journals" with no names
Does the ISSN match?Search it at the ISSN portalName or ISSN doesn't match the site
Is the indexing real?Search the journal in the database it claims, such as DOAJ or PubMedClaimed, but not found
Who publishes it?Find the publisher in COPE or OASPA member listsNo identifiable publisher or address
Who reviews?Ask how many reviewers per paper and how they're chosen"Fast" or "easy" review, or a promise of acceptance
Who owns it?Ask whether the program founded, funds or edits itProgram staff edit it, and the site doesn't say so
Are the editors real?Check two names on their own university pagesThey don't mention the journal
What will it cost?Get every fee in writingFees appear only after acceptance

For a conference, run the same idea. Has it been held before? Can you name the society that organizes it? Do the listed speakers mention it on their own pages? Who publishes the proceedings, and where are they indexed?

If the program can't answer these in writing, that's an answer. Our post on whether paid research internships are worth it has more questions to ask about the program itself, and this one covers checking the professor.

How do you describe it honestly if you've already published?

Say what it is. "Paper in a student research journal" or "preprint" is accurate and safe. Don't upgrade it to "peer-reviewed publication" unless you can say who reviewed it. ProPublica notes that preprint platforms don't peer review at all.

Then make the work speak for itself. Be ready to explain the question, the method and the weakest result in five minutes, with no notes. A reader may skim the line on your application, but an interviewer or a mentor's letter can't be skimmed.

The short version

A legitimate journal survives checks it doesn't control: the ISSN portal, the index it claims, its reviewers and its editors. If the program owns the journal or promises acceptance, know that before you pay, and describe the result honestly afterward.

Related questions

Is a journal predatory just because it charges a fee?

No. Many legitimate open access journals charge authors a fee. The warning signs are fees that aren't stated clearly, fees paired with little or no review, and promises of acceptance.

What if the program runs its own journal?

That's a conflict of interest you should know about. Ask directly whether the program founded, funds or edits the journal, and describe the publication honestly on any application.

Does a student journal count on a college application?

We found no published guidance on how admissions offices weigh student journals. ProPublica reported that a first reader often takes 10 minutes or less per application, and that MIT's dean of admissions told it the mentor's recommendation gets more attention than the research itself.

Is a preprint the same as a publication?

No. Preprint platforms post papers before or without peer review. A preprint is fine to list, but call it a preprint.
